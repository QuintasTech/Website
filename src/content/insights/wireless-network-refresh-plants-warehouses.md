Wireless networks in plants and warehouses carry far more than they were designed for. Handheld scanners, tablets, vehicle-mounted terminals, autonomous mobile robots, cameras and sensors all depend on Wi-Fi that may have been installed for a handful of laptops years ago. When coverage drops, picking stops and production waits.

A refresh is a good time to design for what the site needs now and over the next five to seven years.

## Why industrial Wi-Fi is hard

- **Metal and moving stock.** Racking, machinery and changing inventory levels reflect and absorb signals. A site that worked when shelves were empty may fail when they are full.
- **High ceilings.** Access points mounted high need the right antennas to put coverage where people and devices are.
- **Roaming.** Devices on forklifts and robots move constantly and need to hand off between access points without dropping sessions.
- **Legacy devices.** Older scanners may only support older standards and security settings.
- **Interference** from industrial equipment and neighboring networks.

## Start with a proper survey

A predictive design in software is a good starting point, but in industrial environments it should be followed by an on-site survey with test access points in representative locations, ideally when the site is fully stocked. Survey again after installation to validate.

## Choosing a standard

- **Wi-Fi 6 (802.11ax)** is now the baseline for new deployments and handles high device density well.
- **Wi-Fi 6E** adds the 6 GHz band, which offers more clean channels but has shorter range and requires client support.
- **Wi-Fi 7** adds further capacity and features such as multi-link operation. It suits sites expecting heavy growth, but check client device support, since many industrial handhelds lag behind.

In practice, the client devices you will use over the next few years should drive the decision.

## Design for security and segmentation

- Separate wireless networks (SSIDs and VLANs) for corporate users, operational devices, guests and IoT.
- Use WPA3 where devices support it, and WPA2-Enterprise with certificate-based authentication for managed devices.
- Keep OT and IoT devices out of the corporate network and route their traffic through appropriate firewalls.
- Retire legacy security settings as old devices are replaced.

## Don't forget the wired side

New access points may need more power than existing switches provide over Ethernet (PoE), and faster uplinks. Check switch capacity, cabling and power budgets as part of the design.

## Manage it centrally

Cloud-managed or centrally managed platforms make it easier to run consistent configurations across many sites, monitor performance and troubleshoot remotely.
