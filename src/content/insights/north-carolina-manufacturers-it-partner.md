North Carolina has one of the largest manufacturing sectors in the country, from food and beverage, textiles and furniture to pharmaceuticals, automotive components and advanced materials. Plants across the Research Triangle, the Piedmont Triad, Charlotte and rural communities are investing in automation, data and new facilities.

That investment depends on IT that works on the plant floor as well as in the office. Many manufacturers find that generic IT providers are not equipped for it. Here is what to look for.

## Understanding of operational technology

A manufacturing IT partner must understand that production systems are different: PLCs, SCADA, historians and MES have different priorities, vendors and change processes from corporate IT. They should be able to secure and connect those systems without disrupting production, and they should know when to defer to your plant engineers.

## Multi-site capability

Most manufacturers operate several plants and warehouses, often in different states. Look for standard designs that can be repeated across sites, central management and monitoring, and the ability to get engineers on site when needed.

## Local presence with national reach

When a plant network fails, remote support goes only so far. A partner with people in North Carolina can be on site quickly at plants across the state. At the same time, they should support your sites elsewhere in the US with the same standards.

## Breadth across the stack

Manufacturing IT spans plant networks, data center and edge infrastructure, ERP and business applications, cloud, security and data. A partner who can work across all of these reduces handoffs and finger-pointing between providers.

## Security that fits manufacturing

Ransomware has hit manufacturers hard. Your partner should understand IT/OT segmentation, secure remote access for equipment vendors, backup and recovery for plant systems, and frameworks such as ISA/IEC 62443 and NIST CSF. Customers in automotive, aerospace, defense and pharmaceuticals may also impose specific requirements, such as CMMC for defense supply chains.

## Vendor neutrality

Plants accumulate equipment from many vendors. A partner tied to a single manufacturer's products will struggle to support a mixed estate objectively.

## Questions to ask

1. Which manufacturing clients and environments similar to ours have you supported?
2. How do you handle changes to production systems?
3. How quickly can you get an engineer to each of our plants?
4. Who will design our solution, and will they stay involved?
5. How do you secure your own remote access into our environment?
