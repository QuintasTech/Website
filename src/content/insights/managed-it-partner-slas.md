Handing part of your IT operations to a managed services provider (MSP) can free your team for higher-value work, extend coverage to 24 hours a day and give you access to skills that are hard to hire. It can also go badly, usually because expectations were never written down. Here is what a good arrangement looks like.

## Be clear about scope

The most common source of friction is disagreement about what is included. A good statement of work lists:

- **The estate covered.** Which servers, network devices, cloud subscriptions, endpoints and applications, ideally tied to a maintained inventory.
- **The activities.** Monitoring, patching, backup checks, incident response, change implementation, user support, vendor management.
- **What is excluded.** Projects, new deployments and work on systems outside the inventory, and how those are priced.
- **Who does what.** A responsibility matrix (often called a RACI) that shows which tasks the provider owns, which you own and which are shared.

## The service levels that matter

Many contracts quote impressive-sounding numbers that measure the wrong thing. Ask for these:

| Measure | What it tells you |
| --- | --- |
| Response time by priority | How quickly a qualified engineer starts working, not just how fast a ticket is acknowledged |
| Resolution or restoration target | How quickly service is restored for each priority level |
| Availability of key services | Uptime of the systems the provider manages, measured monthly |
| Patch compliance | Percentage of managed systems patched within the agreed window |
| Backup success and restore testing | Not only that backups ran, but that restores were tested |
| Customer satisfaction | Feedback from your users after tickets close |

Priority definitions should be agreed in advance. A production line down or an outage affecting many users should be Priority 1 with a response measured in minutes, any time of day.

## Reporting and governance

Expect a monthly service report covering SLA performance, ticket trends, recurring problems, risks and recommendations. Hold a quarterly review with someone senior from the provider to discuss improvements, upcoming changes in your business and the roadmap. An MSP that only talks to you when something breaks is not managing anything.

## Security responsibilities

Be explicit about who is responsible for security monitoring, vulnerability management, and responding to incidents. Ask how the provider protects its own access to your environment: named accounts, MFA, privileged access tools and logging. Providers are an attractive target precisely because they hold keys to many customers.

## Exit terms

Good contracts include a transition-out plan: documentation, credentials and knowledge transfer on termination, with reasonable notice periods. It is easier to agree this at the start than at the end.

## Red flags

- Service levels that measure acknowledgement rather than action.
- No named service manager.
- Vague scope ("all IT support") without an inventory.
- Reluctance to share runbooks or documentation.
- Price far below the market, which usually means corners will be cut somewhere.
