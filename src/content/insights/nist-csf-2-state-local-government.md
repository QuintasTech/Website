The NIST Cybersecurity Framework (CSF) is the most widely used structure for organizing a cybersecurity program in the United States. Version 2.0, released in February 2024, broadened its scope beyond critical infrastructure to organizations of every size and sector, including state and local government. For agencies with limited staff and budgets, it offers a practical way to assess where they stand and decide what to do next.

## What the framework is

The CSF is not a regulation or a checklist. It is a common language for describing cybersecurity outcomes, organized into six **functions**:

- **Govern** (new in 2.0): setting strategy, roles, policy and oversight, including supply chain risk.
- **Identify:** understanding assets, risks and priorities.
- **Protect:** safeguards such as access control, training and data security.
- **Detect:** finding attacks and anomalies.
- **Respond:** acting on detected incidents.
- **Recover:** restoring services and learning from incidents.

Each function breaks down into categories and subcategories describing specific outcomes.

## What changed in 2.0

- **Govern became its own function**, reflecting that cybersecurity is a leadership and risk-management responsibility, not only a technical one.
- **Supply chain risk management** received much more emphasis, important for agencies that rely heavily on vendors and cloud services.
- **Implementation examples and quick-start guides** were added to make the framework easier for smaller organizations to apply.
- **Organizational profiles** were clarified, making it easier to describe your current state and target state.

## How agencies can use it

1. **Build a current profile.** Assess each category honestly: what outcomes are you achieving today?
2. **Define a target profile.** Based on your risks, obligations and resources, decide what you need to achieve over the next two to three years.
3. **Prioritize the gaps.** Not every gap matters equally. Focus first on those that reduce the most risk, such as MFA, backups, asset inventory and incident response planning.
4. **Map to your obligations.** Many requirements agencies already face, such as CJIS Security Policy for criminal justice information, IRS Publication 1075 for federal tax information and HIPAA for health data, map to CSF categories. Use the framework to see them in one place.
5. **Report progress to leadership** in the language of the six functions, which non-technical officials can follow.

## Funding and support

Programs such as the State and Local Cybersecurity Grant Program and free services from CISA, including vulnerability scanning and assessments, can help agencies fund and execute their plans. A CSF-based assessment is often a useful foundation for grant applications.

## Start small

An agency does not need to address every subcategory at once. A one-day facilitated workshop with IT, leadership and key departments can produce a current profile and a short list of priorities.
